Foreign Gifts to US Universities

Grouping, sorting, and a flow diagram — the same three moves, on a real disclosure dataset
Understanding Uncertainty · in-class group work

US universities must disclose foreign gifts and contracts over $250,000 to the Department of Education. This activity uses the real disclosure records — 28,221 individual gifts — to practice exactly the summary tools from today's lecture: groupby, sorting, and value counts, then a genuinely different way to see a categorical relationship: the flow (alluvial/Sankey) diagram, which shows an association between two categorical variables — who gave, and who received — instead of just each variable alone.

Money vs. count of gifts

Sum answers "who gives the most money." A separate question — "who gives the most often" — needs value_counts() instead of sum(), and gives a genuinely different ranking:

counts = (
    df['Country of Giftor']
      .value_counts()
      .nlargest(20)
)
Country of GiftorNumber of gifts

Qatar doesn't even reach this table, despite topping the total-dollars ranking by a wide margin — its giving is concentrated in a small number of very large gifts, not a steady stream of small ones. England gives the most often but doesn't crack the total-dollars top 8. Same dataset, same groupby-and-sort recipe, two entirely different pictures depending on which aggregation (sum vs. count) you ask for.

Flows: giftor → institution

Think · Pair · Share · a new kind of plot
  • Think · 2 minA bar chart shows one categorical variable's totals. What does a bar chart of "top countries" or "top institutions" alone fail to show you about how money actually moves through this system?
I've done the Think step — reveal Pair & Share
  • Pair · 3 minA bar chart can't show which giftor sent money to which institution — it only shows each categorical variable's own marginal total, never the pairing. That pairing is a joint distribution over two categoricals.
  • Share · 2 minAgree on what kind of plot could show a joint relationship between two categorical variables, then look at the Sankey diagram below.

A bar chart of country totals and a bar chart of institution totals are both just the marginal distributions — each categorical variable summarized on its own, with the pairing between them thrown away. A Sankey (alluvial) diagram plots the joint distribution instead: one node per giftor and per institution, one flow per (giftor, institution) pair, with flow width proportional to total dollars.

import plotly.graph_objects as go

giftor, recipi, flow = 'Giftor Name', 'Institution Name', 'Foreign Gift Amount'
flows = (
    df.groupby([giftor, recipi])[flow]
      .sum()
      .nlargest(20)
      .reset_index()
)

labels = list(dict.fromkeys(flows[giftor].tolist() + flows[recipi].tolist()))

fig = go.Figure(go.Sankey(
    node=dict(label=labels),
    link=dict(
        source=flows[giftor].map(labels.index),
        target=flows[recipi].map(labels.index),
        value=flows[flow],
    ),
))
fig.show()

The same recipe works at the country level instead of the individual-giftor level — swap giftor = 'Country of Giftor' — to see which countries' money concentrates in which universities.

Qatar's $2.7B is overwhelmingly three giftors — a single national foundation and its two sub-programs. China's much smaller $1.2B splits across many more distinct, mostly institutional giftors (universities, holding companies), with no single giftor coming close to a majority share. Two countries can have wildly different total-dollar rankings for structurally different reasons: one concentrated funder versus many diffuse ones.

FAQ

Why does a Sankey diagram need dict.fromkeys instead of just a list of unique labels?

dict.fromkeys(a_list) removes duplicates while preserving first-seen order — a plain set() would also deduplicate but scramble the order, which would silently break the labels.index(...) lookup that maps each giftor/institution name to its node position.

Why filter to the top 20 flows instead of plotting all 28,221 gifts?

A Sankey diagram becomes unreadable well before 28,221 flows — the visual point is to see the handful of dominant relationships, not to encode every row. This is the same "K largest" instinct as nlargest(20) in the summary tables above, just applied to a joint distribution instead of a single variable.
