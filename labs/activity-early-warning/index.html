Spatial Early Warning from County Outages

Do Virginia's power outages spread between neighboring counties, or does each county fail on its own?
Understanding Uncertainty · in-class group work

EAGLE-I power-outage data for all 132 Virginia counties and independent cities, at hourly resolution across all of 2022, gives a real testbed for exactly the question a Markov chain answers: does the next state depend only on the current state, or does it depend on something more — here, on what neighboring counties are doing? This activity compares three stories, in order of how much structure each one assumes.

Q1 · Independent county outage processes

The simplest possible story: counties do not talk to each other. Each county i has its own fixed outage probability \(p_i\), estimated as the fraction of training hours (the first 70% of the year) where that county has an outage event. Simulating forward is one Bernoulli draw per county per hour, with no memory and no neighbors:

p_iid = Y_train.mean(axis=0)

def simulate_iid(steps, p):
    return (rng.random((steps, len(p))) < p).astype(int)

The counties with the highest independent event rates are exactly the ones you'd guess from population and grid complexity:

If this model is right, outage maps over time should look like spatial white noise: no clustering, no spreading, just each county's own coin flip every hour.

Q2 · A Granovetter-style tipping model

Now let neighboring counties matter. An inactive county can still activate on its own, but its probability of activating goes up with how many of its neighbors are currently active — the same logic as Granovetter's threshold model of collective behavior, applied to a power grid instead of a riot or a fad:

$$P(\text{outage at } t+1 \mid \text{inactive at } t) = \min\bigl(\text{base}_i + k_i \cdot \text{neighbor\_fraction}_{i,t},\ 0.9\bigr)$$

\(\text{base}_i\) is estimated directly from how often county \(i\) turns on with zero active neighbors; \(k_i\), the contagion coefficient, is chosen by grid search to minimize Brier score (mean squared error between the predicted probability and the observed 0/1 outcome) on the training period. A county with \(k_i = 0\) behaves exactly like the independent model from Q1; a large \(k_i\) means that county's risk is dominated by what its neighbors are doing.

Fairfax has both a high independent rate (\(p_i \approx 0.44\) in Q1) and a high contagion coefficient (\(k_i = 0.20\), tied for the highest in the dataset). What would it look like if a county had a high independent rate but a low \(k_i\) instead? Is that a "worse" county to live in, or just a differently risky one?

Show one way to think about it

High \(p_i\), low \(k_i\): that county fails often, but on its own — a local infrastructure problem, not a regional-weather or shared-grid problem. High \(k_i\) means the county's own risk is genuinely coupled to its neighbors, so a warning system for that county should watch its neighbors, not just its own history.

Q3 · A six-hour early-warning policy

A warning system has to look forward. Define a simple neighbor-count rule: warn an inactive county at hour \(t\) if at least \(K\) of its neighbors are currently active, and score that warning against whether the county actually has an outage event sometime in the next 6 hours (the HORIZON), evaluated on the held-out test period (the last 30% of the year):

$$\text{warn}_{i,t} = \mathbb{I}\{\#\text{active neighbors of } i \text{ at } t \ge K\}$$

The tradeoff is stark and immediate: requiring just \(K=1\) active neighbor already misses 66.8% of real outages within the 6-hour window (a 66.8% false-negative rate) while still generating warnings 10.3% of the time when nothing is coming. Raising \(K\) to 3 or higher makes false alarms nearly disappear — but the false-negative rate is already above 97.7%, meaning the policy has essentially stopped warning about anything at all. There is no threshold in this table that is simultaneously low-false-alarm and high-recall; that tradeoff is the entire content of Q3.

Extension · using the fitted tipping risk as the warning score

The neighbor-count rule from Q3 is deliberately crude — it only asks "how many neighbors," not "how risky is this county to begin with." The fitted tipping model from Q2 gives a continuous, county-specific risk score instead:

$$\text{risk}_{i,t} = \min\bigl(\text{base}_i + k_i \cdot \text{neighbor\_fraction}_{i,t},\ 0.9\bigr)$$
risk = np.minimum(base_onset + k_hat * neighbor_fraction_matrix(test_current), 0.9)

for cutoff in np.linspace(0.01, 0.30, 30):
    warn = (risk >= cutoff) & test_inactive
    # ...same TP/FP/FN/TN bookkeeping as Q3, now varying a probability cutoff
Try it

Sweep the cutoff from 0.01 to 0.30 and plot false-positive rate against false-negative rate (an ROC-style curve). Does the fitted risk score dominate the plain neighbor-count rule from Q3 — i.e., can you find a cutoff that beats every \(K\) in the table above on both error rates at once?

Show what to expect

The fitted score should weakly dominate the neighbor-count rule, because it uses strictly more information (each county's own base rate and contagion strength, not just a raw neighbor count) — but the fundamental tradeoff from Q3 doesn't disappear, it just shifts to a more favorable curve. There is still no cutoff that gets both error rates near zero simultaneously.

FAQ

Why estimate on a training period and evaluate on a held-out test period?

Fitting \(base_i\) and \(k_i\) on the same hours used to score the policy would let the model "memorize" the exact outages it's being tested against, inflating its apparent performance. The 70/30 train/test split makes the evaluation a genuine out-of-sample test, the same logic behind every train/test split in this course.

Why does the false-negative rate rise so quickly as K increases?

Most Virginia counties have relatively few neighbors (typically 4–11), and most outages don't involve many simultaneously-active neighbors at once — so requiring a high neighbor count filters out almost every real event along with almost every false alarm. The neighbor-count rule runs out of "evidence" long before it runs out of counties to warn about.
