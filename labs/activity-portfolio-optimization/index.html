Portfolio Optimization by Formula and Monte Carlo

Monte Carlo is most convincing when you already know the answer it should find
Understanding Uncertainty · in-class group work

The CARA-normal portfolio problem has a closed-form solution — which makes it the perfect place to check that a Monte Carlo optimizer actually converges to the right answer before trusting it on a harder problem that has no formula at all. This activity solves the same allocation problem three ways: analytically, by simulation, and — once realistic long-only constraints are added — numerically, using real monthly returns on three ETFs.

2 · CARA-normal analytic solution

Let \(w\) be the risky-asset weights, with the rest of wealth in the safe asset, so terminal wealth is \(W_1 = 1 + r_f + w\cdot(R - r_f)\). Under CARA utility with normally distributed returns, maximizing expected utility is equivalent to maximizing the certainty equivalent:

$$CE(w) = 1 + r_f + w\cdot(\mu - r_f) - \frac{a}{2}\, w^\top \Sigma w$$

which has a clean closed-form unconstrained optimum (here with risk aversion \(a=8\)):

$$w^* = \frac{1}{a}\,\Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f)$$

Negative weights on EFA and EEM are not an error — the unconstrained formula is allowed to short assets and borrow at the safe rate, and with SPY carrying the highest excess return while all three are highly correlated, the formula's best move is to lever up on SPY (weight 1.67, financed partly by borrowing and partly by shorting the other two) rather than hold all three long. Whether that's a portfolio you could actually implement is exactly what Q5 asks.

3 · Monte Carlo optimization

Now pretend the formula is unavailable. Draw 200,000 returns from the fitted multivariate normal, and maximize the simulated certainty equivalent instead — using the log-sum-exp trick to keep the exponential CARA utility numerically stable:

$$\widehat{CE}(w) = -\frac{1}{a}\log\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^M e^{-a\,W_1^{(m)}(w)}\right)$$

At 200,000 draws, the Monte Carlo optimizer lands within a weight-error of \(\|w_{MC}-w^*\|_2 \approx 0.009\) of the true optimum, and the certainty-equivalent loss from using the simulated solution instead of the true one is only 0.00000023 — the simulated optimizer is, for practical purposes, exact here.

Weight error shrinks roughly by half every time \(M\) quadruples — the \(1/\sqrt{M}\) Monte Carlo rate from the WLLN/CLT unit, visible directly in an optimization problem instead of a simple mean. The certainty-equivalent loss shrinks even faster, because the objective is locally flat near its own optimum — a small weight error costs very little utility precisely at the point that matters most.

5 · Adding constraints

Real portfolios usually can't short assets or borrow freely. Impose \(w_j \ge 0\) (no shorting) and \(\sum_j w_j \le 1\) (no borrowing) — the clean inverse-covariance formula no longer applies, but numerical optimization (SLSQP) still solves it directly:

The constrained solution puts everything into SPY alone (73.4% of wealth, rest in the safe asset) and zero into EFA or EEM — the formula wanted to short both of those to lever further into SPY, and once shorting is banned, the corner solution is simply "don't hold them at all." A single dominant, highly-correlated asset plus a safe asset is what CARA-normal optimization does with these three specific ETFs once the unrealistic short/borrow freedom is removed.

6 · The mean-return problem

Historical mean returns are themselves noisy estimates, not the truth — and portfolio optimization is famously sensitive to that noise, since \(w^*\) depends on \(\mu\) directly. Shrink the estimated means toward the safe rate, \(\mu_{\text{shrunk}} = r_f + s\cdot(\mu - r_f)\), and watch the optimal weights move continuously from "everything in the safe asset" (\(s=0\)) to the full historical-mean solution (\(s=1\)):

Try it

Every weight in this table is exactly linear in the shrinkage parameter \(s\) — SPY's weight at \(s=0.4\) (0.669) is exactly \(0.4\times 1.673\). Why does the formula guarantee this linearity, without needing to check it numerically?

Show why

\(w^*(s) = \frac{1}{a}\Sigma^{-1}\bigl(r_f + s(\mu-r_f) - r_f\bigr) = \frac{s}{a}\Sigma^{-1}(\mu-r_f)\) — shrinkage multiplies the excess-mean vector by a scalar \(s\) before it ever reaches the matrix inversion, and matrix-vector multiplication is linear in its input. The entire shrinkage path is just the full-confidence solution scaled by \(s\).

FAQ

Why does the analytic formula allow negative weights at all?

The unconstrained CARA-normal problem has no notion of "short-selling is hard" or "borrowing has limits" built into it — it is a pure calculus optimum over all of \(\mathbb{R}^n\). The formula is mathematically correct for the problem as posed; whether that problem matches what you can actually trade is a separate, practical question, which is exactly what Q5's constraints address.

Why use the log-sum-exp trick for the Monte Carlo certainty equivalent?

CARA utility involves \(e^{-aW}\), and with \(a=8\) and wealth values that can be large or very negative across 200,000 draws, computing that exponential directly overflows or underflows in floating point. logsumexp computes \(\log\sum e^{x_i}\) in a numerically stable way by factoring out the largest exponent first, giving the exact same mathematical answer without the overflow.
