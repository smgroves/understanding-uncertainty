# Understanding Uncertainty — Reading Roadmap (Aug 25 – Oct 1, 2026)

*A prep-reading map for the current schedule. Two books carry the actual reading load: Larry Wasserman's* All of Statistics *(2004) and Blitzstein & Hwang's* Introduction to Probability *(2nd ed., 2019) — wherever either has a real chapter or named section on the topic, it's listed as Primary or Supporting. Casella & Berger's* Statistical Inference *and Efron & Hastie's* Computer Age Statistical Inference *(CASI) are cited the same way — as Primary/Supporting — specifically where one of them has a full chapter that Wasserman/B&H don't (survival analysis and the bootstrap both live only in CASI). Beyond that, intuition and in-class visuals are drawn from Grinstead & Snell's* Introduction to Probability*, CASI, Gelman/Hill/Vehtari's* Regression and Other Stories *(ROS), and David Spiegelhalter's* The Art of Statistics *— the last for in-class display, not assigned reading. Every chapter/section/figure number below was confirmed against an actual copy — six PDFs on hand (Wasserman, Blitzstein & Hwang, Grinstead & Snell, CASI, Casella & Berger, and Penguin's official sample of Spiegelhalter) were read directly; ROS was checked against its official public companion site (`avehtari.github.io/ROS-Examples`) rather than a local copy, since no local copy exists and the only one findable online was an unauthorized upload I deliberately did not use. (Two copies of Blitzstein & Hwang sit in the Knowledge Base folder — one plainly named, one with a piracy-site tag in its filename; I read only the plain one.) Nothing here touches the site or the source repo; this is a planning document only, and it lives only in this `Understanding Uncertainty` folder.*

**How to read the table:** "Primary" and "Supporting" each combine Wasserman and Blitzstein & Hwang (B&H) — the two default reading tracks, prefixed inline so you can tell which is which; read whichever author you prefer, or both. "C&B / CASI (fuller treatment)" is for topics where neither Wasserman nor B&H has the material at all, or where you want more depth than either gives — three of our topics (survival analysis, the bootstrap, standard errors) are genuinely fuller in CASI than in either primary text, so those get elevated here rather than staying a footnote. "Intuition" points to a specific passage that explains the idea in plain language *before* the formalism — a different passage from whatever the same book already contributes elsewhere in the row. "Visual supplements" are specific, real figures worth pulling up during class — Spiegelhalter's are marked **(sample)** where free, or **(full book)** where not. "Notebook source" is the actual `uu_fa26` file the class is built from.

## Two honest gaps — one filled by B&H's appendix, none fully

**Vectors and inner product (8/27) and data wrangling (8/25) aren't in any of these six texts as a *taught* topic.** All of them assume linear algebra and basic data-handling as prerequisites. B&H's Appendix A.3 does define matrix multiplication, transpose, and eigenvectors mechanically, so I've listed it as a Supporting reference — but it never builds the length/distance/covariance-as-dot-product intuition this class centers on, so it's a partial exception at best. The class notebooks remain the primary source for both; a standard linear-algebra reference (Strang) would still be a better second source than any of these six if you want one.

**Survival function and hazard rate (9/17) isn't in Wasserman, B&H, or Casella & Berger** — none of the three has a survival-analysis chapter. It *is* a full chapter in CASI (Ch. 9), which is why it's promoted to the "fuller treatment" column rather than left as a footnote.

**KDE (9/15) is the mirror-image case**: Wasserman covers it (Ch. 20) but none of the other five sources do, since none of them is a density-estimation text.

## If you want a gentler on-ramp before Wasserman/B&H

The chapter guide already in `_Knowledge Base/textbooks/Understanding Uncertainty Prep/` notes that Wasserman's Part I (Chapters 1–5) covers roughly the same ground as **Grinstead & Snell**, Chapters 1, 2, and 4–9, at about a third of the pace and with much more derivation. For 9/1 and 9/22–10/1 specifically, Grinstead & Snell is worth having as a slower-paced backup for any student who finds either primary text too terse on a first pass.
