The Notation Dictionary

The math in this course reuses a small alphabet of tricks — capitalize it, put a bar over it, put a hat on it — and each trick means the same thing everywhere it appears. Once you can read the accent instead of just the letter, a page of unfamiliar symbols turns back into a page of familiar ideas. Each card below gives one notational device a plain-English rule and something to touch.

01

X vs. x — random variable vs. value

Capital X is the rule that turns an outcome into a number, before you know what happens. Lowercase x is the one number that rule actually produced, once the outcome is known. Here, X(s) = +1 if the die shows odd, −1 if even — roll it and watch X resolve into x.

X = "+1 if odd, −1 if even" — a rule, not a number yet.

02

Xi vs. xi — a sequence of draws

The subscript i just names which draw in a sample you mean. Before you collect data, (X₁, X₂, …, X₅) are five not-yet-observed random variables. After you roll five dice, (x₁, x₂, …, x₅) are five fixed numbers — the capital letters become lowercase the moment you look.

Five slots, none observed yet — each one is still the random variable Xᵢ.

03

X̄n vs. x̄n — the sample mean, random vs. realized

X̄ₙ is a formula — (X₁+⋯+Xₙ)/n — applied to data you haven't drawn yet, so it's random: draw a different sample and you get a different average. x̄ₙ is what that formula equals for the one sample you actually collected — a single fixed number. Every dot below is one hypothetical x̄ₙ; together they trace out X̄ₙ's own distribution, scattered around the true μ = 3.5.

04

Bar vs. Hat — X̄ vs. θ̂

An overbar always means the same thing: the arithmetic average of the data underneath it. A hat means something broader: "our best guess at an unknown, fixed truth" — it doesn't have to be an average at all. Every bar is wearing a hat (an average is one way to estimate something) but not every hat is a bar.

Bar — X̄ₙ

The sample mean of wages, X̄ₙ = (1/n)Σxᵢ. Literally an average of the raw data.

Hat, not a bar — β̂

A fitted regression slope, β̂. It estimates the true unknown slope β, but it comes from a least-squares formula — no single column of numbers is just being averaged.

05

μ and σ² — population parameters

Greek letters mark the true, fixed numbers describing the whole process — not something computed from a sample. μ = E[X] is the population mean, σ² = V[X] is the population variance. They never wear a bar or a hat, because nothing is being estimated yet — they're the hidden target that X̄ₙ and s² are trying to hit.

The six faces of a fair die are equally likely — but until you peek, treat μ and σ² as unknown.

06

s² vs. σ² — estimating the population variance

σ² is the true variance you never observe directly. s² is your data-based guess at it — but since you don't know the true μ, s² has to plug in x̄ₙ instead, which quietly shrinks the answer. Toggle the divisor below on the sample {2, 4, 6} and watch which one lands on the truth.

sample = {2, 4, 6}  ·  x̄₃ = 4  ·  Σ(xᵢ − x̄₃)² = 8
s² = 8 / 3 = 2.667 (divide by n)

Dividing by n undershoots on average — this is exactly Bessel's correction.

07

E[X] and V[X] — operators, not functions of one value

The square brackets mean the operator acts on the whole random variable at once, weighting every outcome by its probability, and returns a single number. E[X] is the probability-weighted average outcome; V[X] is the probability-weighted average squared distance from that average. This game pays $0, $3, or −$2, each with probability ⅓.

08

pℓ, F(x), f(x) — mass, cumulative, density

Lowercase pℓ (or p(x)) is a literal probability for one outcome. Capital F(x) is the cumulative probability P(X ≤ x) — capital here means "add everything up to here," not "the random variable." Lowercase f(x) is a density: never a probability by itself, only its area is. The capital/lowercase rule flips depending on which corner of the alphabet you're in — that's the trap.

Discrete die faces: bars are pℓ, the orange staircase is F(x).

09

Σ — read the summation, not just the symbol

Σ means "add up one copy of what follows for every value the index takes." The label under and over it — ℓ = 1 to L, or i = 1 to n — just names which index and how far it runs; swapping ℓ for i or j changes nothing, since it's a dummy variable that disappears once the sum is done. Unroll E[X] = Σℓ=16 pℓxℓ for a fair die below.

E[X] = Σℓ=16 pℓxℓ = p₁x₁ + p₂x₂ + p₃x₃ + p₄x₄ + p₅x₅ + p₆x₆
= (⅙·1) + (⅙·2) + (⅙·3) + (⅙·4) + (⅙·5) + (⅙·6) = 3.5

10

(𝒮, ℰ, p) — the probability space underneath X

Everything above is built on three ingredients. 𝒮, the state space, is every possible outcome. ℰ is the collection of events — subsets of 𝒮 you're allowed to ask a probability of. p assigns each of those events a number in [0, 1]. A random variable X is just a function riding on top of this triple, turning an outcome in 𝒮 into a number.
